The thesis

Crypto's promise is self-custody.
Self-custody's foundation is hardware.

A private key is just a number — but whoever knows that number owns the money. Keep it on an internet-connected computer and it lives alongside every browser tab, extension, and background process you've ever trusted. One phishing page, one malicious update, one infostealer, and the number is gone. Not frozen. Not disputed. Gone.

The industry's first answer was to hand keys back to custodians — and custodians became the single points of failure crypto was invented to remove. Mt. Gox, FTX, and a decade of frozen withdrawals made the lesson expensive: a key someone else holds is a promise, not property.

The durable answer is architectural. Move the key to a device that has one job: hold secrets and sign transactions. No browser. No app store. No general-purpose operating system for malware to live in. The key is generated on the device, never leaves the device, and every transaction is confirmed on the device's own screen with your own thumb. That is the device layer — and it's the only layer of the stack an attacker can't reach through a wire.

1 job: keep keys and sign — nothing else runs on the device
0 times a private key crosses the wire — signatures travel, secrets don't
100% of firmware open source and auditable, built deterministically

The stack

Know which layer you're trusting

Every crypto interaction passes through three layers. Each one fails differently — and only one of them fails safe.

Layer 3

Application layer

Wallet apps, exchanges, dapps, browser extensions. The most useful layer — and the most attacked. Interfaces can be spoofed, updates hijacked, sessions drained. Assume it is compromised, and design so that it doesn't matter.

Fails by: phishing · malware · supply-chain attacks

Layer 2

Protocol layer

Chains, consensus, smart contracts. Remarkably resilient in aggregate, but contracts have bugs, bridges get drained, and governance can change the rules underneath you. You can verify it — you just can't control it.

Fails by: contract bugs · bridge exploits · governance capture

Layer 1

The device layer

Purpose-built signing hardware. Keys are generated here, live here, and die here. Every outbound transaction is displayed on a screen malware can't draw on and approved by a button malware can't press. It's the layer where you are the consensus.

Fails only by: physical access + your PIN — and you'll know

Principles

What a device-layer product must be

Not every gadget with a screen qualifies. These are the tests we hold KeepKey to — and that you should hold any hardware wallet to.

Keys never leave

Generated on-device from hardware entropy, stored on-device, used on-device. Only signatures ever cross the wire.

Trusted display

The device's own screen shows the real address and amount. What you approve is what gets signed — no host software in between.

Physical confirmation

Nothing moves without a button press. A remote attacker can steal your session — they can't steal your thumb.

Open source, top to bottom

Closed firmware is a custodian in disguise. Every line is public, and deterministic builds prove the binary matches the code.

No operating system

No OS means no processes, no downloads, no attack surface for keyloggers and viruses. The layers malware infects simply don't exist.

Recoverable by you alone

A standard BIP-39 seed restores everything onto any compliant device. Lose the hardware, keep the money. No account. No permission slip.
